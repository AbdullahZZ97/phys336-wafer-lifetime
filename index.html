Grading a Solar Wafer: Lifetime and Photoluminescence

PHYS 336 · Physics of Semiconductor Devices · KFUPM, Term 261 · Course project, Idea 4 · Abdullah Alsultan
Engineering question. How does a solar factory know — without touching a wafer — how good a solar cell it will make? Answer in one line: light the wafer, read how many excess carriers it holds (by photoconductance or by its photoluminescence glow); that number sets the quasi-Fermi-level split, which is the voltage the finished cell can reach.

1 · Why a factory grades wafers

A solar factory turns billions of silicon wafers into cells every year. The wafer's minority-carrier lifetime τ — how long a light-made electron survives before it recombines — fixes the best voltage any cell made from it can reach. Measuring τ before the expensive steps (diffusion, passivation, metallisation) lets the factory reject, price or sort each wafer, and catch a bad furnace or ingot within minutes.

The trade-offs this project answers

  • Speed vs. absolute accuracy: photoconductance gives an absolute, injection-resolved lifetime of one spot; PL imaging gives a whole-wafer map in under a second but needs calibration (Tab 6).
  • Thickness: a thinner wafer holds a higher Δn for the same light (less volume to recombine in) but is more sensitive to its surfaces, τs ≈ W/2S (Tab 2).
  • The physical limit: even a perfect wafer loses carriers by Auger and radiative recombination — no wafer exceeds ≈ 0.76 V (Tabs 5, 6).

2 · Excess carriers under light: lifetime and quasi-Fermi levels

Generation

Each absorbed photon with hν > Eg makes one electron–hole pair. Spread evenly through the thickness (assumption; valid when L ≳ W, see below):

G = X · Jph / (e W) (X = suns)

Steady state

G = R(Δn) = Δn / τeff(Δn)  ⇒  Δn = G τeff solved numerically (bisection)

This is the PS6 rate equation dΔn/dt = G − Δn/τ in steady state. Because τeff depends on Δn (Auger, surface diffusion), the page solves it self-consistently.

Quasi-Fermi levels and the implied open-circuit voltage

n = ni e(EFn − EFi)/kT,   p = ni e(EFi − EFp)/kT  ⇒  np = ni² e(EFn − EFp)/kT Neamen 6.4
iVoc = (EFn − EFp)/e = (kT/e) ln[(Na + Δn) Δn / ni²] p-type, n0 ≪ Δn

In the dark np = ni²: one Fermi level, zero voltage. Light pushes np above ni²; the split is the largest voltage the cell's contacts can deliver. At low injection (Δn ≪ Na) iVoc ≈ (kT/e) ln(Na G τeff/ni²): every decade of τ is worth kT ln10 = 60 mV.

Trade-off: implied Voc vs. thickness for several S (τSRH and Jph fixed). Thin wafers win only if their surfaces are passivated.

3 · Photoconductance: the flash, the coil and the reference cell

Sinton & Cuevas (1996) [1]: place the wafer on an RF coil and fire a photographic flash. The extra carriers raise the conductance, which loads the coil's oscillating field (eddy currents) — measured without contacts. A calibrated reference cell beside the wafer records the light, giving G(t). The ratio gives τeff over a whole sweep of Δn in one flash.

Δσ W = e Δn (μn + μp) W sheet photoconductance
τeff = Δn / (G − dΔn/dt) generalized [8]
  • Quasi-steady-state (QSS): τ ≪ flash decay ⇒ dΔn/dt ≈ 0 ⇒ τ = Δn/G.
  • Transient: after the flash, G = 0 ⇒ τ = −Δn/(dΔn/dt) (PS6 Problem 1).
  • Generalized: keeps both terms; valid for any τ [8].

Instrument sourced

Sinton WCT-120: QSSPC and transient photoconductance modes; lifetimes from 10 ns to > 10 ms; implied Voc vs. illumination (implied I–V) without contacts; samples up to 16 × 16 cm² (WCT-120MX: 230 mm wafers) [13].

Pitfalls an examiner may ask about

  • Subtract the dark conductance first (PS6 1(c)(iii)): only Δσ decays to zero; σ0 = e(μnn0 + μpp0) does not. The coil's dark reading is zeroed before the flash.
  • Mobility sum μn + μp must be known at the doping and injection level; carrier–carrier scattering lowers it at high Δn.
  • Trapping and depletion-region modulation add extra conductance at low Δn and make τ look too long [18, 23]. PL imaging is immune to both [2].
  • Optical factor: the fraction of light the wafer absorbs (coating, texture) sets G; it is an input, here Jph.

4 · Photoluminescence: why bright means good

The radiative channel of Tab 2 emits a photon for each event. Its rate is B(np − ni²), so the glow measures np directly — the same product that sets the voltage (Trupke et al. 2006 [2]; generalized Planck law [20, 21]):

IPL ∝ B (np − ni²) W ≈ B (Na + Δn) Δn W
IPL(E) ∝ α(E) E² e−(E − Δμ)/kT,   Δμ = EFn − EFp = e·iVoc ⇒ iVoc = (kT/e) ln(IPL/C)

Ten times brighter = 60 mV more voltage. A camera therefore photographs a voltage map of the wafer. The constant C (optics, camera, reflection) is found once by comparing with a photoconductance measurement [19]. Researchers use exactly this to track wafers: ΔVoc ≈ (kT/e) ln(IPL/IPL,0) [26].

Shape of the room-temperature PL spectrum (simplified phonon-assisted model). Peak ≈ 1.10 eV ≈ 1120 nm (measured ≈ 1130–1140 nm).

How the image is taken

  • Excite with a near-IR laser or LEDs (above Eg) over the whole wafer.
  • A long-pass filter blocks the excitation; the camera sees only the ≈ 1.1 µm glow.
  • Silicon CCDs barely see 1.1 µm (the wafer is nearly transparent there for the same reason), so cooled Si or InGaAs cameras are used.
  • Exposure ≈ 1 s or less: fast enough to run inline [2, 16].
  • Dark regions = short lifetime: dislocation clusters, grain boundaries, impurity rings, edges.

Answer. Put PL imaging inline on every wafer — it is the only method fast enough to see every wafer and it shows where the defects are — and keep a QSSPC tester offline to calibrate the PL signal into absolute Δn and implied Voc and to measure τ(Δn). The two are complementary: same physics (Δn), two read-outs.

Effective lifetime needed vs. target voltage (this wafer's Na, W, Jph). Red: the most any wafer can have (Auger + radiative only).

Low injection: τreq = ni² eeV/kT / (Na G) — ten times more lifetime for every 60 mV. Near 0.75 V the required Δn becomes comparable to Na, Auger recombination (∝ Δn², Δn³) rises faster than G can supply, and the requirement crosses the intrinsic ceiling: that crossing is the voltage limit of silicon (≈ 0.76 V, [10]).

Conclusions

  1. The factory reads the excess-carrier density under light; through np = ni² e(EFn−EFp)/kT it is a voltage before any junction exists.
  2. At low injection each decade of τeff is 60 mV; surfaces must be passivated (S ≲ 10 cm/s) or they, not the bulk, set τeff.
  3. Photoconductance is absolute and injection-resolved; PL imaging is spatial and fast; production uses PL inline, calibrated by photoconductance.
  4. No silicon wafer can exceed ≈ 0.76 V: intrinsic Auger and radiative recombination. Today's industrial PERC cells (0.6875 V per cell) are ≈ 70 mV below it; the best laboratory cells (≈ 0.74–0.75 V) are within 15–20 mV [11].

Assumptions, valid range, and where the model stops being reliable

  • assumption T = 300 K, kT = 0.026 eV (course constants). Module data are at 25 °C: −2 mV/K ⇒ ≈ +4 mV for 25 °C vs. 27 °C.
  • assumption Uniform Δn through the thickness. Valid when Leff ≳ W; the page warns when L < W (then the real Δn piles up near the lit face).
  • assumption SRH lifetime independent of injection (true for Δn ≪ Na; real defects such as Fe or B–O change τ with Δn).
  • assumption Generation from Jph spread evenly; no reflection or parasitic absorption beyond Jph.
  • assumption Mobility independent of injection (carrier–carrier scattering ignored) — overestimates Δσ above Δn ≈ 10¹⁶ cm⁻³.
  • assumption No band-gap narrowing and Brel = 1 in the Richter model: valid for Na ≲ 10¹⁷ cm⁻³ and Δn ≲ 10¹⁷ cm⁻³.
  • limit Doping range 10¹⁴–10¹⁷ cm⁻³, p-type only, non-degenerate (Boltzmann statistics, Neamen 4.3).
  • limit Implied Voc is a property of the wafer, not of a finished cell: emitter, contacts, shading and series resistance are not modelled.
  • limit PL image: synthetic defect map; no lateral diffusion blur, photon reabsorption or camera response.
  • limit Flash: ideal exponential decay; no trapping or depletion-region modulation in the simulated photoconductance.
